## Verification status — read this before citing anything below

| Flag | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **[FULL]** | The paper or page itself was fetched and read |
| **[ABS]** | Abstract or arXiv listing read directly |
| **[SNIP]** | **Seen only in a search-result snippet.** The claim attributed to it has *not* been checked against the source. |
| **[2ND]** | Known only via a secondary source (blog, aggregator, marketing page) |

**Most entries below are [SNIP] or [2ND].** That is not a defect in the research — it is the
honest state after one session — but it means:

> **No [SNIP] or [2ND] source may be cited in a publication, an ADR's `[cited]` line, or any
> public claim until it has been fetched and read.** Promote the flag when you do, and record
> the date.

Numbers are the highest-risk category. Several figures below come from Medium posts, DEV
articles and vendor marketing pages that were themselves summarising a paper. **Treat every
percentage as unverified until read at source.**

Suggested workflow: `docs/10-research/sources/` for fetched PDFs (gitignored — do not
redistribute copyrighted papers), with this file recording the promotion.

Read in full (v3) 2026-08-19. Their result is a strict **generalisation** ("cascade routing", optimal w.r.t. the quality/cost *estimators*), not literally a continuum — the continuum reading comes from their noise-grid finding that which paradigm wins varies smoothly with (σ_ante, σ_post). **Two facts that matter for ADR-0002:** (1) they *do* estimate estimator variance per deployment on a validation set and fit hyperparameters per deployment — so "the routing literature treats signal noise as given" is too strong; (2) on SWE-Bench they **assume the ground-truth test oracle is perfect** — β = 0 by assumption — and their theory is silent on systematically biased verifiers. No critical noise threshold is derived anywhere (their MAX-DEPTH=3 is a runtime cap, not a derived quantity). **Consequence: state the novelty claim as (a) binary/asymmetric false-accept formulation for external verifiers, (b) per-repository measurement, (c) depth/parallelism derived from it — not as "estimator noise governs the routing choice", which this paper already has in Gaussian form.** |

| [FULL] | **Max Schemmer et al., "Appropriate Reliance on AI Advice: Conceptualization and the Effect of Explanations", ACM IUI 2023. arXiv:2302.02187. https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.02187 (read via ar5iv full render)** Defines Appropriateness of Reliance (AoR) as a TWO-dimensional construct and gives the formulas now most widely reused: Relative AI Reliance RAIR = (cases where the human was initially wrong, advice was right, and they switched) / (all cases where human wrong + advice right); Relative Self-Reliance RSR = (cases where human initially right, advice wrong, and they held firm) / (all cases where human right + advice wrong). Measured result: LIME feature-importance explanations raised RAIR from 29.59% to 38.87% (p=.05) but left RSR statistically unchanged, 71.87% to 69.45% (p=.54). Change in self-reported confidence fell from 0.19 to 0.06 (p=.02). *Population:* n=200 recruited on Prolific (199 analysed), crowdworkers, not domain experts. Task: hotel-review deception detection, binary classification. AI was an SVM at 86% accuracy. *Limits:* Explanations increased the rate of FOLLOWING the AI without improving the ability to REJECT it — i.e. they moved reliance, not discrimination. Establishes nothing about generative or agentic AI, nothing about multi-step artefacts, and nothing about experts. The RAIR/RSR construction structurally requires the human's independent pre-advice answer plus ground truth; neither is normally observable in an agentic coding workflow. Transfer to AI coding agents: low without a forced pre-commitment step. |
| [FULL] | **Helena Vasconcelos, Matthew Jörke, Madeleine Grunde-McLaughlin, Tobias Gerstenberg, Michael S. Interact. 7, CSCW1, Article 129 (2023). arXiv:2212.06823. https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.06823 (read via ar5iv full render)** Over-reliance (defined as the percentage of decisions in which a human accepts an AI's incorrect prediction) behaves as a rational cost-benefit response to cognitive effort, not as a fixed cognitive bias. Five studies, N=731 total. Easy mazes: ~18% over-reliance with or without explanations. Hard mazes: ~70% (prediction only) vs ~68% (explanation) — explanation made no difference. Written explanations vs prediction-only: ~68% vs ~66% on hard, i.e. no effect. The one manipulation that worked was making verification cheap: a salient explanation that surfaced the error dropped over-reliance from ~69% to ~28%. Raising the monetary bonus did essentially nothing (~58% vs ~57%). *Population:* Prolific crowdworkers across five studies (n=340, 340, 286, 114, 76). Task: solving 10x10 to 50x50 mazes with an AI's proposed path — a task with cleanly manipulable verification cost and unambiguous ground truth. *Limits:* An artificial task chosen precisely because verification cost is dial-able; real code review is not. The paper does NOT show explanations reduce over-reliance in general — it shows over-reliance tracks the cost of checking, and most explanations do not lower that cost. The negative incentive result is important: paying people more to be right did not make them check. Transfer to AI coding agents: the mechanism (verification cost) transfers well conceptually; the magnitudes do not. |
| [FULL] | **Yunfeng Zhang, Q. Vera Liao, Rachel K. E. Showing the model's confidence score DID shift behaviour (participants switched to the AI more often on high-confidence cases, p=.035). Showing local explanations did NOT calibrate trust — no evidence of any advantage over baseline (p=.66). Critically, neither produced a joint-performance gain: no significant difference in AI-assisted accuracy across the prediction and confidence conditions. Human-alone accuracy 65%; AI 75%; no complementarity achieved. The authors attribute this to aligned error boundaries — the cases the AI found hard were the cases the humans found hard. *Population:* n=72 Amazon Mechanical Turk workers (Experiment 1), plus 9 in a follow-up. Task: income prediction (>$50K) from the 1994 Census Adult dataset. AI accuracy 75% by stratified sampling across confidence bands. *Limits:* Small n, crowdworkers, tabular binary classification, 2020-era model. The aligned-error-boundary explanation is the more dangerous finding for any harness: if the checks and the human fail on the same inputs, calibrating the human buys nothing. Nothing here tests generative output or code. Transfer to AI coding agents: the specific numbers do not transfer; the 'calibrated trust without performance gain' dissociation is the durable lesson. |
| [FULL] | **Zana Buçinca, Maja Barbara Malaya, Krzysztof Z. Gajos, "To Trust or to Think: Cognitive Forcing Interventions Reduce Overreliance on Explainable AI", Proc. ACM Hum.-Comput. Interact. 5, CSCW1 (2021). arXiv:2102.09692. https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.09692 (read via ar5iv full render)** Cognitive forcing functions — show the AI only on demand; make the human decide first and then reveal; impose a 30-second delay — measurably reduced over-reliance relative to simple explainable AI (on incorrect AI predictions, 64% over-reliance under simple XAI vs 48% under cognitive forcing, p=.003). The benefit accrued disproportionately to participants high in Need for Cognition, which the authors frame as an intervention-generated inequality. *Population:* n=199 analysed (260 recruited, 49 excluded for poor engagement), US adults on Amazon Mechanical Turk, mean age 37.
